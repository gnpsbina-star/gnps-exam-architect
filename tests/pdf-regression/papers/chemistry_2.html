29. Read the following case study and answer the questions that follow:
The electrolytic conductance of an ionic solution depends on the concentration of ions, temperature, and nature of the solvent. While conductivity (κ) is the conductance of a unit volume (1 cm3) of electrolytic solution, molar conductivity (Λm) represents the conducting power of all the ions produced by dissolving one mole of electrolyte in solution. Kohlrausch examined Λ°m values for numerous pairs of strong electrolytes having common cations or anions, deducing that each individual ion makes a definite independent contribution towards molar conductivity at infinite dilution. This principle enables determination of Λ°m and the degree of dissociation (α) for weak electrolytes such as carboxylic acids, which do not dissociate completely and cannot be evaluated by direct graphical extrapolation.
(i) Why does the specific conductivity (κ) of an electrolytic solution always decrease upon dilution for both strong and weak electrolytes?[1]
(ii) State Kohlrausch's Law of Independent Migration of Ions.[1]
(iii) The electrical resistance of a conductivity cell filled with 0.1 mol L−1 KCl solution is 100 Ω. When filled with 0.02 mol L−1 KCl solution in the same cell, the resistance is 520 Ω. If the conductivity of 0.1 mol L−1 KCl is 1.29 S m−1, calculate the cell constant of the conductivity cell and the conductivity of the 0.02 mol L−1 KCl solution.[2]

30. Read the following passage and answer the questions that follow:
According to the collision theory of chemical reactions, the rate of a chemical transformation depends on the collision frequency (Z) and the fraction of molecules possessing energy equal to or greater than the activation energy (Ea). Svante Arrhenius quantified the temperature dependence of reaction velocity constants by the mathematical relationship k = A e−Ea/RT. A plot of ln k against 1/T yields a straight line with a negative slope equal to −Ea/R. Catalysts alter the reaction pathway by providing an alternate mechanism with a lower activation energy, drastically increasing the fraction of effective molecular collisions per second without changing the standard Gibbs free energy of the overall process.
