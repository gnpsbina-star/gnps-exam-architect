SECTION A — READING SKILLS (20 MARKS)
1. Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions that follow. [10]

1. In a small village on the edge of the Thar desert, water has always been counted, not measured. Grandmothers remember the years by the rain they brought: the year the tank overflowed, the year the well went silent, the year the camels walked two days to drink. For centuries the people of the region built their lives around this scarcity. They dug kunds, shallow circular pits lined with lime, that caught every drop falling on a sloping courtyard. They shared the village pond under rules older than any written law, and nobody, rich or poor, was allowed to bathe in it.

2. When piped water arrived in the 1980s, many of these practices were abandoned within a single generation. The kunds cracked and filled with sand. The pond, no longer needed, became a dumping ground. For a while it seemed that the old wisdom had simply been made unnecessary by engineering. Then the pipes began to run dry for weeks at a time, and the groundwater beneath the village sank lower every summer. The modern system had promised abundance but had quietly depended on a resource it could not replace.

3. The revival began, as such things often do, with one stubborn person. A retired schoolteacher named Kamla Devi persuaded twelve families to clean the old pond before the monsoon. They worked at dawn, before the heat became unbearable, carrying out plastic, broken glass and silt in headloads. Children joined them because it felt like a festival. When the rains came that year, the pond held water for seven months, longer than anyone could remember.

4. Today the village maintains forty-three kunds and two ponds, and the groundwater level has risen by nearly four metres. Visitors come from other districts to study the work, expecting to find a clever new technology. What they find instead is an agreement: each household looks after the catchment nearest to it, and the whole village meets twice a year to decide how much may be drawn. Kamla Devi likes to say that the desert was never short of water; it was short of patience.

5. The lesson travels well beyond the desert. Many of the problems we treat as technical are, at their root, problems of care and cooperation. A pipe can carry water, but it cannot teach anyone to value it. That knowledge has to be passed from hand to hand, the way the grandmothers passed on the names of the rainy years.

(i) Why does the writer say that water in the village was "counted, not measured"? [2]
(ii) Select the option that best describes the effect of piped water on traditional practices.
(a) It strengthened them.(b) It made them seem unnecessary.
(c) It had no effect on them.(d) It made them illegal.
(iii) Complete the sentence: The revival of the pond became possible because ______. [1]
(iv) What did visitors expect to find in the village, and what did they actually find? [2]
(v) Explain Kamla Devi's statement that the desert "was short of patience". [2]
(vi) Find a word from paragraph 2 that means "left behind or given up". [1]

2. Read the following passage and the chart, and answer the questions that follow. [10]

1. A survey of 1,200 secondary school students in four Indian cities asked how they spend the first hour after school. The largest group, 38 percent, said they use a phone or tablet for entertainment. Twenty-four percent go straight to tuition classes, and 18 percent play an outdoor sport. Only 11 percent read for pleasure, while the remaining 9 percent help with work at home.

2. Researchers noted a sharp difference between students who spend that hour outdoors and those who spend it on a screen. Students who played a sport reported sleeping on average forty minutes longer and said they felt less anxious before examinations. Those who read for pleasure scored higher on a vocabulary test, even when their school marks were similar to those of their classmates.

3. The researchers do not suggest that screens are harmful in themselves. Their point is simpler: the first hour after school sets the tone for the evening, and small choices made then add up over a school year. A student who reads for twenty minutes a day, they calculate, finishes roughly fifteen books by the end of the year, and one who plays outdoors builds habits of fitness and teamwork that last well beyond school.

(i) What percentage of students read for pleasure in the first hour after school? [1]
(ii) What two benefits did students who played a sport report? [2]
(iii) Why do the researchers say that the first hour after school "sets the tone for the evening"? [2]
(iv) Which group scored higher on the vocabulary test, and why is that finding significant? [2]
(v) Identify the main idea of paragraph 3. [1]
(vi) Infer one reason why students might choose a screen over an outdoor sport. [2]
